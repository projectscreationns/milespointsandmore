Something That's Bothering Me Right Now

RantFinanceWarning

FDIC insured. Four words that are supposed to mean your money is safe. Right now they mean absolutely nothing, and it's doing my head in.

Remember 2023?

March 2023. Silicon Valley Bank went down after punters pulled $42 billion out in a single day. Third biggest bank failure in American history. Signature Bank went days later, First Republic a few weeks after that - three of the four largest bank collapses ever, inside two months.

Bank stocks got smashed. First Republic dropped 52% in a morning. And the whole country suddenly gave a toss about one thing: is my money FDIC insured?

Fair enough too. 88% of SVB's deposits sat above the $250,000 insured limit. People got a very fast, very public lesson in what happens when you're over the line.

From that moment on, everyone checked for the FDIC badge. And every fintech in the world noticed.

So they all slapped it on

Suddenly every app had 'FDIC insured' plastered across the landing page. Nice interface, 5% interest, big green tick, safe as houses.

Except most of them were never banks. There's a law from 1933 that says you can't take deposits without a bank charter. The workaround came out of the PayPal days - don't hold the money yourself, partner with a real bank and let them hold it. Regulators agreed that made PayPal not-a-bank. Every neobank since has run through that same gap.

So Yotta wasn't a bank. It was an app sitting on a middleman called Synapse, which sat on a small Memphis bank called Evolve - eight branches, and the back end for over a thousand fintech companies.

In April 2024 Synapse went bankrupt. Not the bank. The middleman.

The middleman failed, not the bank. The FDIC never triggered, because it only pays out when a bank collapses. No bank collapsed.

And then it got worse

Here's the part that actually made my jaw drop. Synapse didn't open a bank account for each person. They chucked everyone's money into a handful of giant pooled accounts, and Synapse were the only ones keeping track of who owned what.

The bank just saw one big pile. Only Synapse held the list of whose money was whose. Synapse died, and the list died with it.

And they'd been losing track for years. A leaked email from November 2022 - eighteen months before the collapse - has an Evolve employee telling Synapse the balances “tend to differ a couple hundred million on the daily.” A couple hundred million. Daily. Nobody stopped.

So when it fell over, roughly $100 million was simply missing. People sent Evolve their statements showing what they'd saved. Evolve ran its own numbers and paid out what it reckoned it owed:

Others got $17. Or $20. Two years on people are still locked out - one bloke is still owed $23,419.21 and counting. This wasn't play money either: emergency funds, house deposits, entire family savings. The fallout was missed mortgage payments, repossessed cars, emptied retirement accounts.

And when SVB went down, every depositor got made whole even above the $250k limit. These people got nothing. The difference? They weren't rich enough to matter.

And the influencers

These apps didn't blow up on their own. They got shoved down everyone's throat by influencer cunts recommending shit products for affiliate money.

Same script every time - 'better than a bank', 'FDIC insured', 'why is nobody talking about this'. Nobody read the fine print because nobody got paid to read the fine print. They got paid per signup.

Then it collapsed, people lost their savings, and those same accounts rolled straight onto the next sponsored product without a single follow-up video. California fined Yotta a million dollars for claiming accounts were FDIC insured. A million. Against $100 million of other people's money. Cost of doing business.

If someone's paid per signup, they're not giving you advice. They're a salesman with a ring light.

So what about all these high yield savings accounts?

Everyone's got a HYSA now, and I get asked about them constantly. Here's the thing nobody says: the rate is the marketing, the structure is the product. Two accounts can both advertise 4.5% and be completely different animals underneath.

There are really only two kinds:

A real bank offering a HYSA
Fine
they hold your money, they have the charter, FDIC applies directly
An app offering a HYSA
Depends
somebody else holds it - and you need to know who, and who else is in the chain

Yotta was the second kind. So are a lot of the apps being pushed right now. That doesn't automatically make them a scam - plenty work fine for years - but it does mean the FDIC badge on the homepage is describing someone else's licence, not theirs.

SoFi, since everyone asks

SoFi is the one I get asked about most, and credit where it's due: SoFi went and got an actual bank charter. They bought a small California bank in early 2022 and became SoFi Bank, N.A. - a nationally chartered, FDIC-insured bank, regulated like any other. It's on the FDIC register. That's a completely different situation to Yotta, and it's the right way to do it.

One wrinkle worth knowing. SoFi Bank itself insures you to the standard $250,000. If you want the headline $3 million of coverage they advertise, that comes from the SoFi Insured Deposit Program, which spreads your balance across a network of other partner banks. It's a documented, regulated programme - not a Synapse situation - but it does mean once you go past $250k your money is sitting at banks whose names you'd have to go looking for.

For most people parking an emergency fund well under $250k, SoFi is a real bank holding your money. Which is exactly the question you should be asking about every single one of these.

And the rate itself

Don't get attached to it. HYSA rates are variable - the bank can drop it the week after you move your money and there's nothing you can do. Chasing 0.3% between accounts is a waste of your life. Getting the structure right matters far more than squeezing the last few basis points.

Pick the account on who holds the money. Treat the rate as a bonus that can vanish, because it can.

Check it yourself - it takes 30 seconds

This is the bit I actually want you to do. There are free government registers that tell you whether the thing holding your money is a real, licensed bank. Search the name. If it's not on there, it's not a bank.

One catch, and it's a big one: the register tells you the bank is real. It can't tell you whether there's a Synapse-style middleman sitting between you and it. There's no public register for that, which is exactly the hole in the system.

My own near miss

I opened a Go2Bank account a while back. Never put a cent in, and thank god.

The whole thing felt off from the first screen. What finished it was trying to contact them - there was nobody to ring. Chat only. That's it. I'm about to hand over money and there isn't one human on the end of a phone.

I walked. Any outfit that wants your deposits but won't give you a phone number has already told you what you're worth to them.

Where I sit on it

I'm not anti-fintech. I use plenty of it and I'll chase a bank bonus all day - there's a stack of them on this site right now.

But the emergency fund doesn't live in an app. That sits at a real bank I can walk into. Chase the bonus with money you could afford to have frozen for two years, and keep the stuff you'd actually cry about somewhere boring.

And when a deal goes up here it's because I've looked at it, not because someone paid me to.

Bottom line: if you can't name the bank holding your money, you haven't got a bank account. You've got an app and a hope. Go check the register.
